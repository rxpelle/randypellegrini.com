The Crusades gave fiction its most morally loaded battlefield: two civilizations, three religions, and a narrow strip of coast that everyone involved called holy. Two centuries of war produced sieges, betrayals, impossible alliances, and at least one leper king — and yet the era is weirdly underwritten compared to, say, the Tudors, who mostly just executed each other.

I spent three years in this period writing Book 6 of my series, which means I read everything. These are the ten that are worth your time.

## 1. The Land Beyond the Sea by Sharon Kay Penman (2020)

The Kingdom of Jerusalem in its last golden generation: Baldwin IV, the boy king ruling brilliantly while leprosy takes him apart, the factions circling his throne, and the road that ends at the Horns of Hattin in 1187. Penman's final novel, and the best single book ever written about the crusader states.

Her superpower was always making political history feel like family drama, and here the family is an entire kingdom. Baldwin is one of the great tragic figures in historical fiction — a king who wins every battle and knows he's dying faster than he can win them. If you read one book on this list, read this one.

## 2. Jerusalem by Cecelia Holland (1996)

Rannulf Fitzwilliam, a low-born Templar knight, serves through the same dying years of the kingdom — the leper king's succession, the Templar politics, the catastrophe at Hattin. Holland writes the Templars as they probably were: a military order full of violent, devout, damaged men who had nowhere else to go.

Her prose is stripped to the bone, and her refusal to romanticize anyone — Templar, Saracen, or princess — makes the ending land like a hammer. Criminally out of fashion; find a copy.

## 3. The Book of Saladin by Tariq Ali (1998)

Saladin's story, dictated by the sultan himself to Ibn Yakub, his Jewish scribe — the conquest of Egypt, the unification of Syria, and the campaign that took Jerusalem back. Ali writes from inside the Muslim world looking out at the strange, violent Franks, which is exactly the inversion the genre needed.

The frame matters: a Jewish scribe recording a Muslim sultan's war against Christian invaders, all three traditions tangled in one manuscript. Second book of Ali's Islam Quintet; it stands alone.

## 4. The Split Pomegranate by Randy Pellegrini (2026)

Mine. Damascus, Jerusalem, and the Languedoc, 1140 to 1187 — the years between the Second Crusade and the fall of Jerusalem.

Eschiva bint Yehiel is a Jewish engineer in Damascus who can lay a hand on worked stone or metal and know the maker, the year, the flaw waiting inside it. Brother Guilhem of Carcassonne, a Templar mason, can do the same impossible thing. When they meet across the lines of the Second Crusade, each recognizes in the other the one truth neither can say aloud — and begins a love carried for twenty years in letters and coded seals, while the Order that shelters Guilhem moves to turn an ancient inheritance into a registry of bloodlines it can own.

I wrote it the way Madeline Miller and Anita Diamant write the past: intimate, exacting, lit from within. It's Book 6 of The Architecture of Survival, and it stands entirely on its own.

## 5. Count Bohemond by Alfred Duggan (1964)

The First Crusade through the eyes of its most effective and least pious leader — Bohemond of Taranto, a Norman adventurer who saw a holy war and recognized a business opportunity. Antioch, the starving march, the impossible victories.

Duggan wrote dry, ironic, meticulously researched novels that Evelyn Waugh loved, and this was his last. Nobody has ever written medieval warfare logistics better. The joke of the book is that Bohemond's cynicism keeps being more useful to the crusade than anyone's faith.

## 6. Knight Crusader by Ronald Welch (1954)

Philip d'Aubigny, born in the crusader states — a second-generation Frank who speaks Arabic, bathes regularly, and finds the newly arrived European crusaders half barbarian — fights at Hattin, survives captivity, and ends up in a Wales he's never seen. It won the Carnegie Medal in 1954.

Technically a children's book. In practice it's the best introduction to the era's central irony: the "Franks" of Jerusalem had become Levantine, and the crusades kept being wrecked by newcomers who hadn't.

## 7. The Talisman by Walter Scott (1825)

The Third Crusade: a Scottish knight, a disguised Saladin, a poisoned Richard the Lionheart, and a healing talisman. The history is cheerfully unreliable — Scott admitted as much — but this is the novel that fixed the chivalric Saladin in the Western imagination for two centuries.

Read it as the genre's founding document. Every noble-enemy trope, every desert duel between worthy opponents, every fictional Saladin since — it all starts here.

## 8. Brethren by Robyn Young (2006)

A young Templar sergeant in the 1260s hunts a heretical book stolen from the order's vaults while Sultan Baibars — the Crusades' most underwritten great commander — dismantles the crusader states fortress by fortress. First of a trilogy that runs to the fall of Acre.

Young splits her point of view between Templar and Mamluk, and her Baibars chapters are better than her Templar ones, which I mean as high praise. The late-Crusades period — the long losing endgame — almost never gets fiction this good.

## 9. The Iron Lance by Stephen R. Lawhead (1998)

A Scottish nobleman's son joins the First Crusade and comes home carrying a relic — the lance that pierced Christ's side — and the attention of people who kill for such things. First of Lawhead's Celtic Crusades trilogy.

Lawhead writes the crusade from the cheap seats: not the princes' councils but the mud, fraud, and grinding attrition of the people who actually walked to Jerusalem. The relic-conspiracy spine gives it thriller bones under the pilgrimage.

## 10. The Heaven Tree by Edith Pargeter (1960)

A cheat — it's set in England and Wales under King John, not Outremer. But Pargeter's master mason Harry Talvace, building a cathedral for a patron who is also his destroyer, is the great medieval novel about the people who *build* — and the Crusades era was, above everything, an age of building: castles, walls, water systems, fortress-monasteries.

Pargeter also wrote the Brother Cadfael mysteries as Ellis Peters, and Cadfael himself is a retired crusader. Consider this her answer to what the fighting was for.

## Why the Crusades Still Work

The Crusades are the rare historical setting where every side kept records, every side produced brilliance and atrocity, and every side's descendants are still arguing about it. That moral instability is fatal to propaganda and perfect for fiction. The best books on this list refuse to pick a hero civilization — they pick people, wedge them between the lines, and watch what loyalty costs.

That's where I set mine: two people who belong to enemy worlds and one secret older than both.
