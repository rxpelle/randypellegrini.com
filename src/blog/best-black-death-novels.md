The Black Death killed something like half of Europe in four years. It ended serfdom, broke the Church's monopoly on explanation, and rewired the survivors' relationship to institutions of every kind. It is arguably the most consequential event in the last thousand years of Western history.

And for how enormous it was, the fiction shelf is strangely thin. Plenty of novels use plague as set dressing — a convenient way to empty a castle or orphan a protagonist. Far fewer sit inside the dying and take it seriously.

I write plague fiction, so I've read all of it — the good, the bad, and the merely atmospheric. These are the ten that earn the subject.

## 1. Year of Wonders by Geraldine Brooks (2001)

The village of Eyam, England, 1666: plague arrives in a bolt of cloth from London, and the villagers make an extraordinary decision — they seal themselves in. No one leaves until it burns out. The novel follows Anna Frith, a young housemaid, through the year the village voluntarily dies so its neighbors won't.

The real Eyam did exactly this, and Brooks stays close to the record. What makes the book great isn't the suffering — it's the arithmetic of faith. Every character has to decide, week after week, whether the quarantine is godliness or suicide. Brooks won a Pulitzer later for *March*, but this is her best book.

## 2. Doomsday Book by Connie Willis (1992)

A time-traveling Oxford historian is sent to the fourteenth century by mistake — dropped into 1348 instead of 1320, straight into the Black Death — while an epidemic locks down the Oxford of her own time. Two plagues, seven centuries apart, running in parallel.

It won both the Hugo and the Nebula, and it deserved them. The science fiction machinery is just a delivery mechanism; what it delivers is the most emotionally devastating portrait of the Black Death in fiction. Kivrin arrives knowing exactly what's coming and can save no one. The last hundred pages are as hard as anything I've read.

## 3. World Without End by Ken Follett (2007)

The second Kingsbridge novel, set two centuries after *The Pillars of the Earth*, walks its builders, monks, and merchants directly into 1348. Caris, a merchant's daughter who becomes a nun and effectively a public-health officer, fights the plague with quarantine and soap while the medical establishment prescribes prayer and bleeding.

Follett's move is showing the Black Death as an institutional stress test. The monastery, the guild, the crown — every structure in the book either adapts or breaks. At a thousand pages it's a commitment, but it moves.

## 4. Company of Liars by Karen Maitland (2008)

Nine travelers flee across England in 1348, one step ahead of the pestilence, each carrying a lie. It's *The Canterbury Tales* restaged as a slow-burning horror novel — the plague is the pursuing monster offstage while the travelers' secrets kill them one by one.

Maitland's medieval England is superstitious, wet, and mean, and she refuses to modernize her characters' minds. The dread compounds beautifully. If you want the Black Death as atmosphere thick enough to cut, start here.

## 5. The Aethelred Cipher by Randy Pellegrini (2026)

Mine. Mainz, 1347 — the year the Black Death arrives in the Rhineland, one year before most of the histories say it should.

A dying monk presses an iron key into the hands of Thomas, a young scribe, and the coded trail leads through manuscript marginalia to a secret society that has been tracking plagues — and the people who survive them — since ancient Egypt. The cipher tradition is real: medieval monks genuinely embedded hidden messages in illuminated manuscripts. The plague epidemiology is as accurate as I could make it. The conspiracy is the fiction that ties them together.

I wrote it because I wanted a plague novel where the disease isn't a backdrop — it's an intelligence test, and someone is grading it.

## 6. The Last Hours by Minette Walters (2017)

Dorsetshire, 1348. Lady Anne of Develish quarantines her demesne, pulls two hundred serfs inside the moat, and refuses her dying husband re-entry. Walters spent decades as one of Britain's best crime writers, and she brings a crime writer's discipline to the question everyone else waves away: what does a sealed community eat, and who decides?

It's the practical plague novel — logistics, class, and the discovery that the social order was only ever a habit. First of a two-book series.

## 7. A Journal of the Plague Year by Daniel Defoe (1722)

London, 1665, told as the memoir of a saddler who stayed. Defoe was five years old during the actual Great Plague and wrote this fifty-seven years later from records, interviews, and mortality bills — which makes it either the first historical novel or the first work of documentary fiction, and either way a masterpiece.

The dead-carts, the shut-up houses with watchmen at the doors, the statistical obsession — it reads like reporting. Every pandemic novel written since is quietly downstream of it.

## 8. The Plague by Albert Camus (1947)

Oran, Algeria, sealed off while a plague burns through it, narrated by a doctor who keeps working because the work is what there is. Camus meant it partly as an allegory of occupied France, but he did the epidemiology homework, and the book has outlived its allegory.

Reread in the 2020s, it barely feels like fiction at all — the denial phase, the statistics fatigue, the profiteers, the strange guilt of survivors. The most quotable plague book ever written.

## 9. Hamnet by Maggie O'Farrell (2020)

Stratford, 1596. The plague takes an eleven-year-old boy; four years later his father writes a play called *Hamlet*. O'Farrell's novel barely names Shakespeare — it belongs to Agnes, the mother, and to the grief that the historical record never bothered to write down.

There's a chapter tracing the plague's journey to the boy — flea to monkey to cabin boy to glassmaker's crate to Stratford — that is the single best piece of epidemiological writing in any novel on this list.

## 10. Station Eleven by Emily St. John Mandel (2014)

A flu kills most of humanity in weeks; twenty years later a traveling Shakespeare company walks the Great Lakes performing for the settlements that remain. Not medieval — but no list of plague fiction is honest without it, because it's the great novel of what plague is *for* in fiction: finding out what survives.

"Survival is insufficient" is the company's motto, and the book's argument. Every plague novel is secretly about what people carry out of the fire. This one says it out loud.

## Why Plague Fiction Works

Plague is the one catastrophe that tests everything at once — medicine, faith, government, family, and the story a civilization tells about why it deserves to continue. War lets you pick a side. Plague doesn't. That's why the best plague novels are never really about the disease. They're about the institutions that fail, the strangers who don't, and the records someone keeps so the next generation gets a warning.

That last part is where my series lives.
